The [FP8 post](03-fp8-without-hardware.html) stored the *weights* in FP8 and still multiplied in fp16. The GeForce RTX 40 series has tensor cores that multiply FP8 operands directly, so a natural next step is **W8A8**: quantise the activations to FP8 too and run the GEMM on those cores. This post is the feasibility study and its (negative) result.

## What the hardware does

`torch._scaled_mm` calls cuBLASLt's FP8 GEMM. Bare GEMM throughput on this card, sustained, against the fp16 cuBLAS GEMM:

| M x N x K | fp16 | FP8 | speed-up |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| 512 x 4096 x 4096 | 37.0 TFLOP/s | 77.4 | 2.09x |
| 2048 x 4096 x 4096 | 38.0 | 72.6 | 1.91x |
| 4096 x 4096 x 4096 | 37.8 | 72.7 | 1.92x |

Correction (2026-11-21): the first version of this table had an fp16 baseline of 28 TFLOP/s, measured before the laptop GPU had reached its sustained clocks, which inflated the ratios (2.64x at 512 rows). Re-measured with the GPU pre-heated and the two kernels alternating over three rounds: fp16 37 to 38 TFLOP/s, FP8 72 to 77, i.e. **1.9 to 2.1 times**. The 2048 x 11008 x 4096 row was not re-measured and is dropped. The conclusions below do not change.

So the FP8 cores are roughly twice as fast: real, and more than the W4A16 kernels reach at prefill sizes (about 0.85 of Marlin, i.e. about fp16 speed).

## The layer

`FP8W8A8Linear` (`quantize_model(model, "fp8_w8a8")`) stores FP8 E4M3 weights with a scale per output channel and quantises the activations on the fly with a scale per token. Two details cost me an hour each:

* the row-wise scaling mode of `_scaled_mm` is not available on sm_89 (it silently returned wrong numbers in my test), so the product runs with unit tensor scales and the two scale vectors are applied afterwards;
* **the raw FP8 products overflow fp16**: |x| and |w| up to 448 each and K = 4096 give sums near 10^8, so the GEMM must produce f32 and the scales are applied to it; my first version asked for an f16 result and the test printed `inf`.

Not generated by the compiler: LLVM 18's NVPTX backend cannot emit the FP8 `mma` instruction, so this layer is an integration of cuBLASLt, not a QuantForge kernel.

## Quality is fine

Wikitext-2 perplexity on the whole test split: Qwen2.5-0.5B fp16 18.79, FP8 W8A8 19.10 (per-tensor scales: 19.04), INT4 round-to-nearest 22.91. Llama-3.2-1B fp16 17.91, FP8 W8A8 18.13. FP8 keeps relative precision across magnitudes, so even a single scale per tensor works; the cost is about 0.2 to 0.3 perplexity points.

## Speed is not

Prefill of Qwen2.5-0.5B (batch of tokens, one forward pass, best of 10):

| variant | 1 x 2048 tokens | 8 x 512 tokens | perplexity |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| fp16 | 108 ms | 214 ms | 18.79 |
| INT4 (QuantForge) | 127 ms | 255 ms | 22.91 |
| FP8 W8A8, per-token / per-channel scales | 117 ms | 236 ms | 19.10 |
| FP8 W8A8, one scale per tensor | 103 ms | 326 ms | 19.04 |

No gain. Per layer the picture is erratic: for a 2048-token input, the FP8 layer takes from 0.35 to 1.5 times the time of the fp16 layer depending on the shape. Why:

1. **The GEMM is only part of the layer.** The bare FP8 GEMM of a 2048 x 896 x 4864 layer takes 0.24 ms against 0.43 ms in fp16, but quantising the activations (a reduction and an element-wise pass) and rescaling the f32 result (writing 40 MB and reading it back) cost another 0.25 to 0.45 ms. At these layer sizes the overhead is as large as the saving.
2. **Attention, norms and the rest stay in fp16**, and for a 0.5B model they are a large share of the forward pass.
3. The single-scale variant lets cuBLASLt apply the scales inside the GEMM, which removes the rescale pass, but its speed depends on the shape in ways I could not predict (4864 x 896 was three times slower than the per-token variant).

## What it would take

A real speed-up needs the activation quantisation fused into the producer of the activations (or into the GEMM prologue) and the scale epilogue fused into the GEMM store, which is exactly the kind of fusion this compiler does for dequantisation and activation functions. It needs the FP8 `mma` instruction, which is the missing piece in this toolchain. Until then the layer is useful for its **memory** (half the weight bytes of fp16) and its quality, and as a baseline for a future compiler path, not for speed. For batch-1 decode the INT4 kernels remain far better: FP8 weights are twice the bytes of INT4.

## Update: what the INT8 tensor cores give (2026-11-21)

The natural question after the FP8 result is whether the compiler, which *can* emit INT8 `mma` (LLVM 18 has the instruction; it lacks only the FP8 one), could do better. Three measurements.

**1. The hardware.** A register-only microbenchmark of the `mma.sync` instructions (`benchmarks/mma_peak.cu`, 16 warps per SM, no memory traffic), RTX 4050:

| instruction | peak | vs fp16 / fp32 accumulate |
|---|--:|--:|
| fp16 in, fp32 accumulate (`m16n8k16`) | 44.1 TFLOP/s | 1.00x |
| fp16 in, fp16 accumulate | 48.1 | 1.09x |
| **int8 in, int32 accumulate (`m16n8k32`)** | **88.2 TOPS** | **2.00x** |
| fp8 e4m3 in, fp32 accumulate | 96.2 | 2.18x |

cuBLAS fp16 reaches 37 to 38 of the 44 (85 percent). INT8 is a clean 2x, and it is an instruction this compiler can already target.

**2. The library.** `torch._int_mm` first gave 22 to 24 TOPS, *slower* than fp16, which looked like a dead end. It was a layout effect: with B column-major (`weight.t()` of a row-major `[out, in]` weight is exactly that) cuBLASLt reaches **80 to 83 TOPS, 2.1 to 2.2 times fp16**; with a row-major B it silently takes a slow path. A reminder that a library number is a statement about a path, not about the hardware.

**3. The layer and the model.** `INT8W8A8Linear` (per-channel weight scale, dynamic per-token activation scale, cuBLASLt for the product) behaves exactly like the FP8 layer: 1.44x of fp16 for a 2048 x 4096 x 4096 layer, 0.74 to 0.96x for the small layers of a 0.5B model, because the activation quantisation and the scale epilogue cost about 0.4 ms around a 0.86 ms GEMM. Whole-model prefill of 2048 tokens: Qwen2.5-0.5B 95.8 ms against 86.3 ms in fp16 (0.90x), Llama-3.2-1B 166.6 against 178.5 ms (1.07x). Quality is acceptable and matches FP8: wikitext-2 perplexity 19.20 against 18.79 (+0.41) and 18.23 against 17.91 (+0.33).

So the 2x exists in the instruction and in the library GEMM, and is lost in the passes around it. That is the argument for a **compiler-generated** INT8 kernel with the epilogue (row scale, column scale, bias, f16 store) fused into the store, and the activation quantisation fused into the producer of the activations; with those two fused, the arithmetic above suggests about 1.0 ms instead of 1.29 ms for the 4096-wide layer, 1.8x fp16, and a W4A8 variant (int4 weights widened to int8, group scales applied to the int32 partial sums) would also halve the dequantisation work of the current W4A16 kernels. None of that is built yet; it is the most promising way to beat cuBLAS in the compute-bound regime, with accuracy (smoothing / rotation for W4A8) as its main risk.

## Update 2: the compiler's own INT8 kernel (2026-11-21)

The arithmetic of the previous update says the 2x is lost around the GEMM, so the first thing to know is whether the compiler's Tensor Core path reaches anything close to the instruction's peak. I pushed a plain `i8 x i8 -> i32` GEMM (`linalg.matmul_transpose_b`, B as `[N, K]` because `ldmatrix` cannot transpose 8-bit data) through the existing pipeline. It needed three small changes, one configuration discovery and a bug fix:

* the vectorised matmul is `contract(extsi a, extsi b, acc)`, and the nvgpu lowering refuses the `extsi`: LLVM 18's `populateFoldArithExtensionPatterns` folds only `extf`, so the vectorization pass gets a pattern that folds the integer extensions into the contraction (about 20 lines);
* the instruction was `m16n8k16` s8 (no faster than fp16); a new pipeline option `thread-tile-k=32` selects `m16n8k32`;
* the first launch died with `misaligned address`: the shared-memory padding of 8 elements is 16 bytes for f16 but 8 for int8, so a tile row stopped being 16-byte aligned for `ldmatrix`; `smem-pad=16` fixes it;
* the overwrite pass (`accumulate=false`) only knew f32 accumulators and silently kept accumulating into C for i32; it now handles both. I found this because my own test pre-filled the output.
* **the sweep found a real bug**: 48 of 180 block / warp / K-tile combinations gave wrong results (75, 50 or 100 percent of the output), including the ones that looked fastest at 60 TOPS. A per-fragment map of the output showed alternate n8 tiles wrong; the nvgpu IR of a good and a bad configuration was structurally identical, but in the bad one the B tile in shared memory was `memref<64x64xi8>` for a block N of 128. The shared-memory promotion pass recognises operand tiles by shape, `[M-tile x K]` for A and `[K x N-tile]` for B, and a transposed B is `[N-tile x K]`: it was found only when block N happened to equal block M or warp M, and otherwise the pass promoted the *warp* tile into a buffer shared by all warps. A one-line extension of the shape test fixes it; the same sweep now gives 174 exact and 6 clean compile errors (shared-memory budget exceeded), no wrong result, and a regression test covers the combinations that failed.

Result, every output **bit-exact** against cuBLASLt (RTX 4050, GPU pre-heated, overwrite mode, `benchmarks/int8_gemm_probe.py`):

| shape (M x K x N) | compiler-generated INT8 | cuBLASLt INT8 | cuBLAS fp16 | vs fp16 | vs cuBLASLt |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 2048 x 4096 x 4096 | 61.7 TOPS | 69.9 | 38.5 | **1.60x** | 0.88x |
| 4096 x 4096 x 4096 | 64.8 | 77.0 | 38.0 | **1.70x** | 0.84x |
| 512 x 4096 x 4096 | 50.0 | 58.5 | 36.4 | 1.37x | 0.85x |
| 2048 x 4096 x 11008 (swizzle 16) | 59.6 | 77.5 | 38.3 | **1.56x** | 0.77x |
| 2048 x 4864 x 896 | 54.5 | 52.5 | 35.3 | 1.54x | **1.04x** |
| 2048 x 896 x 4864 | 38.6 | 56.1 | 35.1 | 1.10x | 0.69x |
| 2048 x 1536 x 8960 | 51.2 | 64.1 | 36.6 | 1.40x | 0.80x |

Compiled from the same machinery as the W4A16 kernels, with a schedule chosen from a sweep of 129 exact combinations, it reaches 59 to 65 TOPS on the large shapes (three quarters of the 88 TOPS instruction peak, 77 to 88 percent of cuBLASLt, and above it on one shape) and is **1.1 to 1.7 times faster than cuBLAS fp16 on every shape tried**. Weights larger than the L2 want `block-swizzle=16` (2048 x 4096 x 11008: 21.7 to 59.6 TOPS), a reminder that block order matters as much as the tile.

What this is not: a raw int32 GEMM, with no scales, no epilogue and no activation quantisation, so it is not yet a layer and not yet a speed-up of anything the user sees. Two caveats: the small-K shapes are held back by the int32 output (4 bytes per element), which a fused f16-store epilogue would halve; and it is one GPU, sm_89. The next steps are the ones the arithmetic named: fuse the row / column scales and the bias into the store, and quantise the activations in the kernel that produces them; then W4A8.

## Update 3: fusing the dequantisation, and the model (2026-11-21)

A raw int32 GEMM is not a layer: the output has to become f16 with a row scale (the activation's), a column scale (the weight's) and a bias. As a separate kernel that costs the int32 round trip: for a 2048 x 4096 x 4096 layer 33 MB written and read back, about as much time as a quarter of the GEMM. The compiler already folds an activation (relu, silu, swiglu) into the final store of the accumulators; a new epilogue kind, `int8-dequant`, does the same for `y = f16(c * sx[m] * sw[n] + bias[n])`. The program stays semantically ordinary, a `matmul` followed by an elementwise `linalg.generic`; the pass rewrites every `vector<2xi32>` accumulator store into the converted pair, written with one 4-byte store, and erases the elementwise kernel and the int32 buffer. Bit-exact against the f32 reference, with and without bias, and one kernel in the FileCheck test.

**A measurement lesson.** The first benchmark said the fused kernel was *slower* than the raw GEMM (1.93 ms against 1.13 ms for 2048 x 4096 x 4096). `ncu` said it was not (1.21 against 1.25 ms, and half the DRAM writes). The profiler's device time (1.09 ms) against the wall time per launch (2.05 ms) located the missing millisecond on the host: the int32 buffer's allocation had survived the rewrite because dead `subview`s still pointed at it, so every call did a `cudaMalloc` and a `cudaFree` of 33 MB. Erasing the dead subviews removed it. Only comparing device time with wall time showed that the kernel was never the problem.

GEMM plus dequantisation, one launch, f16 out (RTX 4050, GPU pre-heated; the fp16 column is a plain GEMM without any scale):

| shape (M x K x N) | cuBLAS fp16 | int32 GEMM only | fused GEMM + dequant | vs fp16 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 2048 x 4096 x 4096 | 1.70 ms | 1.05 | 1.10 | **1.54x** |
| 4096 x 4096 x 4096 | 3.66 | 2.18 | 2.02 | **1.81x** |
| 2048 x 4096 x 11008 | 4.92 | 3.12 | 3.00 | 1.64x |
| 2048 x 4864 x 896 | 0.51 | 0.34 | 0.31 | 1.61x |
| 2048 x 1536 x 8960 | 1.55 | 1.07 | 0.96 | 1.62x |
| 2048 x 896 x 4864 | 0.51 | 0.45 | 0.40 | 1.29x |
| 512 x 4096 x 4096 | 0.47 | 0.36 | 0.33 | 1.43x |

The fused kernel is even faster than the raw int32 GEMM, because it writes half the bytes, and the small-K shapes that were the weakest as raw GEMMs now gain the most from the epilogue.

**The model.** `quantize_model(model, "int8_w8a8")` now uses this kernel: activations are quantised per token (one compiled elementwise pass), the weight is per output channel, the GEMM and the dequantisation are the compiled kernel; layers whose N or K do not fit its tile fall back to cuBLASLt. Prefill of 2048 tokens, same run, fp16 against the compiled INT8 (and against the earlier cuBLASLt route):

| model | fp16 | INT8, cuBLASLt route | INT8, compiled fused kernel | perplexity (fp16 -> INT8) |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B | 87.0 ms | 95.5 (0.91x) | 79.3 (**1.10x**) | 18.79 -> 19.20 |
| Llama-3.2-1B | 178.5 | 166.5 (1.07x) | 151.7 (**1.18x**) | 17.91 -> 18.23 |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B | 236.1 | 218.8 (1.08x) | 196.7 (**1.20x**) | 12.66 -> 13.06 |

So the same arithmetic that cuBLASLt could not turn into a whole-model gain is a 10 to 20 percent prefill speed-up when the epilogue is fused in the compiler. It is far less than the 1.5 to 1.7x of the GEMM, and the arithmetic says why: attention, the norms, the head (kept in fp16: for the 0.5B model it is about a sixth of the fp16 time), the activation quantisation pass and the host overhead of a Python layer are not touched. The obvious next steps are fusing the activation quantisation into the producer of the activations, quantising the head, and a W4A8 variant that halves the weight bytes as well; the quality cost (0.3 to 0.4 perplexity points for plain per-token / per-channel INT8) is the price to watch.

## Update 4: sharing the quantisation, a profile, and two measurement traps (2026-11-21)

**Sharing the activation quantisation.** q, k and v read the same activations, and so do gate and up; each layer quantised them again. `quantize_model(..., "int8_w8a8")` now fuses them (a shared-input layer: one quantisation pass, one wider GEMM, each original layer takes its slice). It gains only 1 to 3 percent of prefill (0.5B: 80.6 to 81.1 ms, within the noise; Llama-3.2-1B 155.2 to 151.8; Qwen2.5-1.5B 203.5 to 201.3), so the repeated quantisation was not where the time went. A bug of mine on the way: the shared layer kept the outputs of its last call, about 80 MB per layer for a gate / up at 4096 rows, which ran the model out of memory; they are dropped as soon as every part has been taken, and a test compares the fused and unfused model outputs.

**Where the time goes** (Qwen2.5-0.5B, one 2048-token prefill, kernel device time):

| | fp16 | INT8 |
|---|--:|--:|
| GEMMs of the layers | 41.4 ms | **28.3** (1.46x) |
| `lm_head` (kept in fp16) | 14.3 | 14.3 |
| norms, rotary, silu x up, residual, quantisation... | 24.1 | 31.7 (+7.6 for the quantisation) |
| attention | 6.0 | 5.7 |

The GEMMs really are 1.46x faster; what stops the model from following is that 24 ms, 28 percent of the fp16 prefill, are the element-wise operations of an eager Hugging Face model, which no GEMM kernel touches (an inference engine fuses them), plus a head that a real prefill does not even compute for every position. The quantisation pass itself costs 7.6 ms; `torch.compile` already makes it one kernel at 1.1 times the single-pass ideal, so a hand-written kernel would not help; only folding it into the producer of the activation would (a fused `silu(gate) * up` and quantise, for the input of `down_proj`, is the biggest single piece: about 5 ms here).

**Two measurement traps.** (1) A sweep of `block-swizzle` that timed each variant at a different moment said swizzle helped every layer shape by 3 to 28 percent. Interleaved rounds of all variants in one session said otherwise: it is 2.3x faster for a weight larger than the L2 (2048 x 2048 x 16384: 5.8 to 2.5 ms) and 12 to 14 percent slower for one that fits (2048 x 4096 x 4096), so the rule is now size-based. The clocks of a laptop GPU drift by 10 percent between measurements, more than most of the differences I was trying to measure. The earlier shared-input fusion had exposed it the other way round: with the old threshold the widened gate / up weight of Llama-3.2-1B (33.6 MB, just below the threshold) ran unswizzled at 2.9 times the time and made the model 32 percent slower, until I measured that shape in isolation. (2) The tables in this post come from different sessions; compare a column within a row, never across tables.

Not done, and why: quantising the head (a real prefill needs the last position only), W4A8 (halves the weight bytes as well but needs a per-group int32 to float rescale inside the K loop, a new accumulation scheme in the compiler), and the fused `silu * up` quantisation, which is a kernel for one model family.

## Update 5: W4A8, and the reason I gave for not building it was wrong (2026-11-21)

Update 4 said W4A8 "needs a per-group int32 to float rescale inside the K loop, a new accumulation scheme in the compiler". It does not, if the quantisation is built for it. QServe's *progressive* quantisation does: the weight is first quantised to INT8 with one float scale per output channel, and that INT8 weight is then quantised to INT4 per group of 128 with an **integer** scale (1 to 16) and an integer zero point. The weight the tensor cores need is `(nibble - zero) * group_scale`, which is again an int8, so it can be built in registers while the tile is staged, the accumulation stays one int32 over all of K, and the only float scale left is the per-channel one in the epilogue that already exists. Nothing in the compiler changed.

**The kernel is a producer, not a pass.** The program is a `linalg.generic` that computes the int8 weight from the packed nibbles (`n, k` reads byte `k / 2`, selects the nibble by `k mod 2`, subtracts the group's zero point, multiplies by its scale; all in `i8`), then the `matmul_transpose_b` and the `int8-dequant` store of Update 3. The producer-fusion pass written for the INT4 dequantisation turned out not to be specific to it: any store whose backward slice is pure is recomputed at the loads of the GEMM. It compiled on the first try, bit-exact against the integer reference, in **one kernel** (`W4A8GemmDq`). The weights are the only weight traffic: 4 bits per weight.

**Quantisation, and a quality trap.** My first version used a symmetric 4-bit range per group, and the model got worse than plain INT4 by a lot (Qwen2.5-0.5B perplexity 28.9 against 22.9). I first blamed the integer group scale; choosing it per group by minimum squared error instead of rounding up moved it to 28.0 and Llama-3.2-1B not at all. The ablation that found it replaces the weights by their dequantised values and keeps the activations in fp16: symmetric INT4 with a *float* scale already gives 26.3, asymmetric gives 22.9. The library's INT4 has always used a per-group zero point; I had dropped it to save one tensor. With an integer zero point per group as well (the search keeps only (scale, zero) pairs for which `(q - z) * s` stays inside int8, because the kernel's 8-bit arithmetic wraps rather than saturates) the weights alone give 23.1 and the whole W4A8 layer 23.7 (Qwen2.5-0.5B), 22.06 (Llama-3.2-1B, against 22.28 for INT4 W4A16 with an INT8 head). So the quality is that of INT4, not better.

(The INT4 W4A16 variant of the 1.5B ran out of memory in the Hugging Face benchmark, so there is no number for it; the 1.5B rows of the other variants were run one process each.) The honest summary: **W4A8 is a prefill-speed and quality-neutral replacement of the INT4 W4A16 layer** (1.2x faster for the same bits and about the same perplexity), and it is the smallest format here, but it only reaches fp16 speed at the whole-model level because 28 percent of that prefill is not GEMM; when 1.5 times the memory is affordable, INT8 W8A8 is both faster and 1.6 perplexity points better than W4A8 on the 1.5B (13.06 against 14.70). Decode is untouched (bandwidth bound: the W4A16 kernels already read the same bytes). Weights come from quantising the fp16 model with plain round-to-nearest; GPTQ-style error compensation on top of this format is not done.

Tests: `test_w4a8_quantisation_never_overflows_int8_and_the_linear_matches_its_reference` (the integer weight stays inside int8 for outlier-heavy inputs, the compiled layer equals the integer reference to f16 rounding) and `test_w4a8_shared_input_fusion_gives_the_same_model_output`.

**Update 5b: GPTQ for W4A8.** Round-to-nearest left W4A8 0.3 to 0.8 perplexity points behind INT4 W4A16 on two of three models, so I wrote the GPTQ of this format (`gptq_quantize_weight_w4a8`, `quantize_model_gptq(..., fmt="w4a8")`): the weight is divided by its per-channel scale so that it lives in int8 steps, and for every group of 128 columns the integer (scale, zero point) pair is chosen from the weights *as already updated by the compensation of the earlier columns*; the rest is the usual column-by-column error compensation with the Cholesky factor of the inverse Hessian. Wikitext-2 test perplexity, head in fp16 in every column, 128 calibration windows of 512 tokens:

| model | fp16 | INT4 W4A16 RTN | INT4 W4A16 GPTQ | W4A8 RTN | **W4A8 GPTQ** |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B | 18.79 | 22.91 | 20.45 | 23.72 | **21.06** |
| Llama-3.2-1B | 17.91 | 22.28 | 20.21 | 22.06 | **19.91** |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B | 12.66 | 14.29 | 13.37 | 14.70 | **13.68** |

GPTQ recovers 2.0 to 2.7 points on the small models and 1.0 on the 1.5B, and the quantisation takes 49 to 135 seconds. W4A8 with GPTQ is 0.3 to 0.6 behind INT4 GPTQ on the Qwen models and 0.3 *ahead* on Llama-3.2-1B, where the integer scale with its search happens to fit better; I would not read more into one model. (With an fp16 head INT4 W4A16 of the 1.5B measures 14.29; the earlier table's variant had an INT8 head and ran out of memory in that benchmark.) Layers whose sizes are not multiples of 128 stay dense; `act_order` and the range-shrinking option are not available for this format. Tests: `test_gptq_w4a8_beats_the_w4a8_rtn_on_the_layer_output_error` (output error below 0.8 of RTN, int8 range respected) and an end-to-end tiny-Llama run. `benchmarks/w4a8_gptq.py` reproduces the table.

**Update 5c: the loads of the weight did not vectorise.** The PTX of the W4A8 kernel had 80 scalar `ld.global.u8` per tile where 16 `ld.global.u32` would do. The cause was the way I had written the producer: reading the packed byte through an affine map `k floordiv 2` makes MLIR expand it into a signed floor-division sequence (`cmpi slt`, `subi`, `select`, `divsi`...), and the index simplification pass that lets the load vectoriser see "consecutive bytes" only understands unsigned `divui` / `remui`. Reading with `tensor.extract` at `arith.divui %k, 2` fixed it, without touching the compiler: **the kernel is 7 percent faster**. Kernel, same method as the table above: 2048 x 4096 x 4096 1189 us (58 TOPS), 4096 x 11008 3484 us, 11008 x 4096 3539 us, 896 x 4864 355 us, 2048 x 8192 1187 us, that is 1.19 to 1.40x cuBLAS fp16, 1.33 to 2.02x the W4A16 kernel and 0.74 to 0.86x the INT8 kernel. Whole-model prefill (2048 tokens, one process per variant): Qwen2.5-0.5B 86.4 ms fp16, 76.5 INT8 (1.13x), **83.6 W4A8 (1.03x)**; Llama-3.2-1B 176.5, 149.8 (1.18x), **164.8 (1.07x)**; Qwen2.5-1.5B 231.8, 193.0 (1.20x), **210.9 (1.10x)**; perplexity unchanged. `ncu` says the kernel is now issue-bound with 3.7 times the instructions of the INT8 kernel: the 8-bit arithmetic is still done in 16-bit registers and re-packed with `bfi`. Doing it on the loaded 32-bit word (the nibble product `q * scale` is at most 240, so four bytes never carry into each other, and a SWAR subtract handles the zero point) would cut it about fourfold, but it is a C++ pattern for one kernel and worth roughly 3 percent of a whole-model prefill, so I stopped here.

## Update 6: fusing `silu * up` into the quantisation (2026-11-21)

The profile of Update 4 said the biggest single piece left outside the GEMMs was `silu(gate) * up` followed by the quantisation of `down_proj`'s input, and I had estimated about 5 ms for the 0.5B. The gate / up output is `[rows, 2 x 4864]` in fp16; eagerly, the activation reads it and writes the product (two element-wise kernels), then the quantiser reads the product for the row maximum and again to divide and round. `_silu_mul_quantize` does all of it in one compiled function (float32 inside, int8 and the per-token scale out), and `_fuse_int8` installs it as the MLP's forward when gate, up and down are INT8 or W4A8 layers; with 16 rows or fewer, or a layer the compiled kernel does not take, the generic path runs. Test: the fused model output equals the generic path's for both formats, at 64 and at 8 rows.

The measured gain is twice my estimate. Whole-model prefill of 2048 tokens, each variant in its own process, fp16 re-measured at the end with the GPU warm (89.2 / 182.7 / 240.6 ms; the clocks of this session were 3 percent slower than in the earlier tables, so compare within this table only):

| model | INT8, before | INT8 with the fusion | W4A8, before | W4A8 with the fusion | perplexity (INT8 / W4A8) |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B | 83.3 ms (1.07x) | **73.2 (1.22x)** | 89.0 (1.00x) | **73.6 (1.21x)** | 19.196 -> 19.201 / 23.715 -> 23.714 |
| Llama-3.2-1B | 154.2 (1.18x) | **143.1 (1.28x)** | 169.5 (1.08x) | **157.5 (1.16x)** | 18.233 -> 18.235 / 22.063 -> 22.057 |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B | 199.8 (1.20x) | **177.5 (1.36x)** | 218.4 (1.10x) | **193.8 (1.24x)** | 13.062 -> 13.074 / 14.703 -> 14.693 |

(Ratios against that session's fp16.) So the "28 percent of the prefill is element-wise ops" of Update 4 was not a wall: the part that sits between two quantised layers can be fused, and it was worth 8 to 14 percent on its own. It also changes the comparison of the formats: W4A8, which was at fp16 speed, is now 1.16 to 1.24x of it at a quarter of the weight memory. What is left of the 28 percent are the norms, the rotary embedding and the residual adds; the same trick (fold the quantisation of the next layer's input into the norm that produces it) applies to q/k/v and gate/up, whose input is an RMSNorm output.

## Update 7: the norm quantises its own output, and a fairer baseline (2026-11-21)

q/k/v and gate/up read the output of an RMSNorm, and that output is quantised right after: the norm writes the fp16 result, the quantiser reads it for the row maximum, then again to divide and round. `_rmsnorm_quantize` computes the norm and the int8 values and scales in one compiled function (the fp16 output is still returned, as the module's contract says); `_install_norm_quantisation` makes the `input_layernorm` and `post_attention_layernorm` of a block call it, and the shared layer finds the result through the identity of the tensor the norm returned (a counter, `pre_hits`, lets the test check that the path is really taken: 4 hits per forward above 16 rows, none below). Only the Llama / Qwen / Mistral form of RMSNorm is fused (not Gemma's `1 + weight`).

**A fairer baseline.** Compiling the norm helps *any* model, not only a quantised one, so I measured fp16 with every RMSNorm compiled as well. Whole-model prefill, 2048 tokens, one process per variant, fp16 measured at the start and at the end of each model (the clocks drifted by up to 5 percent inside this session, so only compare within the table):

| model | fp16 eager | fp16, norms compiled | INT8, no norm fusion | **INT8** | W4A8, no norm fusion | **W4A8** |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B | 87.6 / 92.3 ms | 84.3 | 70.6 | **65.8** | 76.1 | **72.7** |
| Llama-3.2-1B | 187.9 / 181.3 | 169.6 | 139.5 | **126.8** | 157.4 | **139.2** |

The fusion is worth 7 to 10 percent for INT8 and 5 to 13 percent for W4A8 on top of Update 6, perplexity unchanged (19.207 against 19.201, 18.238 against 18.235, 13.074 both; W4A8 23.703 against 23.714, 22.069 against 22.057, 14.693 both). Against the mean of the two eager fp16 runs, INT8 is **1.37x, 1.46x and 1.49x** and W4A8 1.24x, 1.33x and 1.34x; against fp16 with compiled norms, which is the comparison I would quote because an engine would not run eager norms, INT8 is **1.28x, 1.34x and 1.43x** and W4A8 1.16x, 1.22x and 1.28x. (The 8 x 512 batches gain even more, for example INT8 Llama 301.9 to 246.9 ms.) Every step since Update 3 was a fusion of something outside the GEMM; what is left of the original 28 percent is the rotary embedding, the residual adds and the attention itself.

## Update 8: a model that does not fit in fp16 (2026-11-21)

Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct is about 6 GB in fp16 and the card has 5.6 GB, so it cannot be loaded at all; every quantised variant below runs on it (weights quantised on the CPU, one layer at a time). ## Update 9: W4A8 inside vLLM (2026-11-21)

`quantization="quantforge_w4a8"` quantises an ordinary fp16 checkpoint to W4A8 while vLLM loads it (no GPTQ checkpoint needed; vLLM builds a quantisation config with no arguments when the method names no config file, so nothing in the model directory has to say anything). The linear layers call one custom op, `quantforge::w4a8_hybrid`: above 16 rows it quantises the activations per token and runs the compiled W4A8 kernel; up to 16 rows it runs the W4A16 decode kernels (K-folding included) on the same weight, written in the other layout (`from_w4a8`: the nibbles repacked along N, the scale `gs * sw` in f16, the zero point unchanged, so the dequantised weights are the same). The branch on the row count is inside the op, which is why `torch.compile` and the CUDA graphs vLLM captures see no Python branch on a dynamic dimension; a test traces the op with `fullgraph=True`.

It ran at the first attempt, and the first measurement was bad: **prefill at half the speed of fp16** (16.6k tokens/s against 31.8k). The cause was the activation quantisation, which I had written eagerly inside the op (about six passes over a float32 copy of the activations, as expensive as the GEMM itself for these sizes); calling the compiled `_int8_quantize_tokens` instead (its first call is vLLM's profiling run, before any graph is captured) made it **33.3k**. vLLM 0.25.1, RTX 4050, 16 prompts of 975 tokens for the prefill, short prompts for the decode, tokens per second, best of three:

| model, method | prefill | decode 1 request | decode 16 requests |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Qwen2.5-0.5B fp16 | 31786 | 152 | 2297 |
| 0.5B **quantforge_w4a8** (online) | **33320** | 257 | 3583 |
| 0.5B GPTQ-Int4, `gptq_marlin` | 32880 | 297 | 4230 |
| 0.5B GPTQ-Int4, `quantforge` (W4A16) | 20622 | 251 | 3524 |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B **quantforge_w4a8** | **12045** | 114 | 1603 |
| 1.5B GPTQ-Int4, `gptq_marlin` | 11517 | 135 | 1998 |
| 1.5B GPTQ-Int4, `quantforge` (W4A16) | 7034 | 117 | 1638 |

The honest reading: the prefill is at the level of fp16 and of Marlin (1.05x on the 1.5B, where the GEMMs weigh more) and 1.6 to 1.7x the W4A16 plugin; the decode is the W4A16 kernels' and so 0.80 to 0.85x of Marlin as before. In a serving engine the GEMMs are a smaller part of the prefill than in the Hugging Face model of the earlier updates (vLLM already fuses the norms and the activation), so the 1.2 to 1.5x there shrinks to parity here. **It is not a clear win over Marlin.** What it offers is that a plain fp16 checkpoint is enough and that the prefill GEMMs use the INT8 tensor cores; what it costs is that both layouts of the weight are kept (about one byte per weight: the memory of INT8, not of INT4), round-to-nearest quantisation (the 0.5B at 23.7 perplexity against 20.5 with GPTQ), and that I have tested it on vLLM 0.25.1 only. Dropping the decode copy (a W4A16 kernel that reads the nibbles along K) or loading GPTQ checkpoints into the W4A8 layout would address the first two; neither is done.

## Update 10: one copy of the weights, and what it costs (2026-11-21)

The hybrid op of Update 9 keeps the nibbles twice, in the W4A8 layout (packed along K, for the prefill kernel) and in the W4A16 layout (packed along N, for the decode kernels): about a byte per weight. To keep only the first, the decode has to read it: an f16 `matmul_transpose_b` whose weight tile is built by the same kind of `linalg.generic` producer (nibble, minus the zero point, times the group scale `gs * sw` in f16), fused into the loads by the pass that was written for something else. It is correct at once (error 0.0046 on outputs of 13.6), but the first schedule was **3.7 times slower** than the dedicated W4A16 decode kernel (188 against 51 us at 16 x 4096 x 4096): that kernel has the packed `f16x2` dequantisation (the trick that puts a nibble in the mantissa of 1152) and the producer here is scalar code. A two- or three-stage pipeline with a 128-deep K tile took it to 87 to 89 us (1.7x); over the layers of a model the slowdown is 1.1x for the wide-K gate / up shape (896 x 4864) to 1.7x (11008 x 4096).

In vLLM that is less than the kernel ratio suggests, because the kernels are only a part of a decode step. `QUANTFORGE_W4A8_DECODE=compact` (default: the two layouts) gives, on vLLM 0.25.1: weights of **1.13 GiB instead of 2.04 GiB** for Qwen2.5-1.5B, prefill unchanged (33.0k and 12.4k tokens/s for the 0.5B and 1.5B), decode 214 and 3245 tokens/s for the 0.5B at 1 and 16 requests against 259 and 3592, and 102 and 1580 for the 1.5B against 114 and 1607. That is 10 to 17 percent at one request and 2 to 10 at sixteen, for half the weight memory. The kernel to close the rest of the gap is a pattern for the packed dequantisation of this producer, as `quantforge-vectorize-dequant` has for the W4A16 one. And a limit I had not stated: this is *online* quantisation, so the whole fp16 model has to fit on the GPU while it loads; Qwen2.5-3B (6 GB) does not on this 5.6 GB card in either mode, although its W4A8 form (2 GB) runs fine through the Hugging Face path of Update 8. Quantising layer by layer while loading, or loading from a pre-quantised checkpoint, would remove it.
