FP8 tensor cores on a laptop GPU: twice the speed on paper, no speed-up in a model

What the Ada FP8 tensor cores deliver for a bare GEMM, an FP8 W8A8 layer built on cuBLASLt, its quality, and why the whole-model prefill is not faster than fp16.

The FP8 post stored the weights in FP8 and still multiplied in fp16. The GeForce RTX 40 series has tensor cores that multiply FP8 operands directly, so a natural next step is W8A8: quantise the activations to FP8 too and run the GEMM on those cores. This post is the feasibility study and its (negative) result.

What the hardware does#

torch._scaled_mm calls cuBLASLt's FP8 GEMM. Bare GEMM throughput on this card, sustained, against the fp16 cuBLAS GEMM:

M x N x K fp16 FP8 speed-up
512 x 4096 x 4096 37.0 TFLOP/s 77.4 2.09x
2048 x 4096 x 4096 38.0 72.6 1.91x
4096 x 4096 x 4096 37.8 72.7 1.92x

Correction (2026-11-21): the first version of this table had an fp16 baseline of 28 TFLOP/s, measured before the laptop GPU had reached its sustained clocks, which inflated the ratios (2.64x at 512 rows). Re-measured with the GPU pre-heated and the two kernels alternating over three rounds: fp16 37 to 38 TFLOP/s, FP8 72 to 77, i.e. 1.9 to 2.1 times. The 2048 x 11008 x 4096 row was not re-measured and is dropped. The conclusions below do not change.

So the FP8 cores are roughly twice as fast: real, and more than the W4A16 kernels reach at prefill sizes (about 0.85 of Marlin, i.e. about fp16 speed).

The layer#

FP8W8A8Linear (quantize_model(model, "fp8_w8a8")) stores FP8 E4M3 weights with a scale per output channel and quantises the activations on the fly with a scale per token. Two details cost me an hour each:

  • the row-wise scaling mode of _scaled_mm is not available on sm_89 (it silently returned wrong numbers in my test), so the product runs with unit tensor scales and the two scale vectors are applied afterwards;
  • the raw FP8 products overflow fp16: |x| and |w| up to 448 each and K = 4096 give sums near 10^8, so the GEMM must produce f32 and the scales are applied to it; my first version asked for an f16 result and the test printed inf.

Not generated by the compiler: LLVM 18's NVPTX backend cannot emit the FP8 mma instruction, so this layer is an integration of cuBLASLt, not a QuantForge kernel.

Quality is fine#

Wikitext-2 perplexity on the whole test split: Qwen2.5-0.5B fp16 18.79, FP8 W8A8 19.10 (per-tensor scales: 19.04), INT4 round-to-nearest 22.91. Llama-3.2-1B fp16 17.91, FP8 W8A8 18.13. FP8 keeps relative precision across magnitudes, so even a single scale per tensor works; the cost is about 0.2 to 0.3 perplexity points.

Speed is not#

Prefill of Qwen2.5-0.5B (batch of tokens, one forward pass, best of 10):

variant 1 x 2048 tokens 8 x 512 tokens perplexity
fp16 108 ms 214 ms 18.79
INT4 (QuantForge) 127 ms 255 ms 22.91
FP8 W8A8, per-token / per-channel scales 117 ms 236 ms 19.10
FP8 W8A8, one scale per tensor 103 ms 326 ms 19.04

No gain. Per layer the picture is erratic: for a 2048-token input, the FP8 layer takes from 0.35 to 1.5 times the time of the fp16 layer depending on the shape. Why:

  1. The GEMM is only part of the layer. The bare FP8 GEMM of a 2048 x 896 x 4864 layer takes 0.24 ms against 0.43 ms in fp16, but quantising the activations (a reduction and an element-wise pass) and rescaling the f32 result (writing 40 MB and reading it back) cost another 0.25 to 0.45 ms. At these layer sizes the overhead is as large as the saving.
  2. Attention, norms and the rest stay in fp16, and for a 0.5B model they are a large share of the forward pass.
  3. The single-scale variant lets cuBLASLt apply the scales inside the GEMM, which removes the rescale pass, but its speed depends on the shape in ways I could not predict (4864 x 896 was three times slower than the per-token variant).

What it would take#

A real speed-up needs the activation quantisation fused into the producer of the activations (or into the GEMM prologue) and the scale epilogue fused into the GEMM store, which is exactly the kind of fusion this compiler does for dequantisation and activation functions. It needs the FP8 mma instruction, which is the missing piece in this toolchain. Until then the layer is useful for its memory (half the weight bytes of fp16) and its quality, and as a baseline for a future compiler path, not for speed. For batch-1 decode the INT4 kernels remain far better: FP8 weights are twice the bytes of INT4.

Update: what the INT8 tensor cores give (2026-11-21)#

The natural question after the FP8 result is whether the compiler, which can emit INT8 mma (LLVM 18 has the instruction; it lacks only the FP8 one), could do better. Three measurements.

1. The hardware. A register-only microbenchmark of the mma.sync instructions (benchmarks/mma_peak.cu, 16 warps per SM, no memory traffic), RTX 4050:

instruction peak vs fp16 / fp32 accumulate
fp16 in, fp32 accumulate (m16n8k16) 44.1 TFLOP/s 1.00x
fp16 in, fp16 accumulate 48.1 1.09x
int8 in, int32 accumulate (m16n8k32) 88.2 TOPS 2.00x
fp8 e4m3 in, fp32 accumulate 96.2 2.18x

cuBLAS fp16 reaches 37 to 38 of the 44 (85 percent). INT8 is a clean 2x, and it is an instruction this compiler can already target.

2. The library. torch._int_mm first gave 22 to 24 TOPS, slower than fp16, which looked like a dead end. It was a layout effect: with B column-major (weight.t() of a row-major [out, in] weight is exactly that) cuBLASLt reaches 80 to 83 TOPS, 2.1 to 2.2 times fp16; with a row-major B it silently takes a slow path. A reminder that a library number is a statement about a path, not about the hardware.

3. The layer and the model. INT8W8A8Linear (per-channel weight scale, dynamic per-token activation scale, cuBLASLt for the product) behaves exactly like the FP8 layer: 1.44x of fp16 for a 2048 x 4096 x 4096 layer, 0.74 to 0.96x for the small layers of a 0.5B model, because the activation quantisation and the scale epilogue cost about 0.4 ms around a 0.86 ms GEMM. Whole-model prefill of 2048 tokens: Qwen2.5-0.5B 95.8 ms against 86.3 ms in fp16 (0.90x), Llama-3.2-1B 166.6 against 178.5 ms (1.07x). Quality is acceptable and matches FP8: wikitext-2 perplexity 19.20 against 18.79 (+0.41) and 18.23 against 17.91 (+0.33).

So the 2x exists in the instruction and in the library GEMM, and is lost in the passes around it. That is the argument for a compiler-generated INT8 kernel with the epilogue (row scale, column scale, bias, f16 store) fused into the store, and the activation quantisation fused into the producer of the activations; with those two fused, the arithmetic above suggests about 1.0 ms instead of 1.29 ms for the 4096-wide layer, 1.8x fp16, and a W4A8 variant (int4 weights widened to int8, group scales applied to the int32 partial sums) would also halve the dequantisation work of the current W4A16 kernels. None of that is built yet; it is the most promising way to beat cuBLAS in the compute-bound regime, with accuracy (smoothing / rotation for W4A8) as its main risk.

Update 2: the compiler's own INT8 kernel (2026-11-21)#

The arithmetic of the previous update says the 2x is lost around the GEMM, so the first thing to know is whether the compiler's Tensor Core path reaches anything close to the instruction's peak. I pushed a plain i8 x i8 -> i32 GEMM (linalg.matmul_transpose_b, B as [N, K] because ldmatrix cannot transpose 8-bit data) through the existing pipeline. It needed three small changes and one configuration discovery:

  • the vectorised matmul is contract(extsi a, extsi b, acc), and the nvgpu lowering refuses the extsi: LLVM 18's populateFoldArithExtensionPatterns folds only extf, so the vectorization pass gets a pattern that folds the integer extensions into the contraction (about 20 lines);
  • the instruction was m16n8k16 s8 (no faster than fp16); a new pipeline option thread-tile-k=32 selects m16n8k32;
  • the first launch died with misaligned address: the shared-memory padding of 8 elements is 16 bytes for f16 but 8 for int8, so a tile row stopped being 16-byte aligned for ldmatrix; smem-pad=16 fixes it;
  • the overwrite pass (accumulate=false) only knew f32 accumulators and silently kept accumulating into C for i32; it now handles both. I found this because my own test pre-filled the output.

Result, every output bit-exact against cuBLASLt (RTX 4050, GPU pre-heated, overwrite mode, benchmarks/int8_gemm_probe.py):

shape (M x K x N) compiler-generated INT8 cuBLASLt INT8 cuBLAS fp16 vs fp16 vs cuBLASLt
2048 x 4096 x 4096 57.2 TOPS 73.0 38.5 1.49x 0.78x
4096 x 4096 x 4096 59.1 79.4 38.2 1.55x 0.74x
512 x 4096 x 4096 48.0 58.9 37.0 1.30x 0.81x
2048 x 4096 x 11008 (swizzle 16) 59.4 75.6 38.1 1.56x 0.79x
2048 x 4864 x 896 50.1 53.3 35.7 1.40x 0.94x
2048 x 896 x 4864 39.3 54.8 35.4 1.11x 0.72x
2048 x 1536 x 8960 46.5 64.0 36.8 1.26x 0.73x

Compiled from the same machinery as the W4A16 kernels, with an untuned schedule, it reaches 57 to 59 TOPS on the large shapes (two thirds of the 88 TOPS instruction peak, three quarters of cuBLASLt) and is 1.1 to 1.6 times faster than cuBLAS fp16 on every shape tried. Weights larger than the L2 want block-swizzle=16 (2048 x 4096 x 11008: 21.7 to 56 TOPS before the overwrite fix, 59.4 after), a reminder that block order matters as much as the tile.

What this is not: a raw int32 GEMM, with no scales, no epilogue and no activation quantisation, so it is not yet a layer and not yet a speed-up of anything the user sees. Three caveats: (1) some tile combinations compile and run but give wrong results on int8 (for example a 64 x 128 block with a 32 x 64 warp tile at K tile 64, found by the sweep because every result was compared with cuBLASLt); the schedule in quantforge.e2e.INT8_GEMM_OPTS is verified, others must be checked; (2) the small-K shapes are held back by the int32 output (4 bytes per element), which a fused f16-store epilogue would halve; (3) one GPU, sm_89. The next steps are the ones the arithmetic named: fuse the row / column scales and the bias into the store, and quantise the activations in the kernel that produces them; then W4A8.
